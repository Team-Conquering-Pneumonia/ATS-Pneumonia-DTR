Project overview

Background

Empiric antibiotics — started at the time of diagnosis, before any bacterial pathogen is confirmed — have long been the standard of care for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). Yet for most patients with CAP no pathogen is ever identified, and the routine availability of viral testing together with the experience of COVID-19 have raised the question of whether every patient with pneumonia benefits from antibiotics. Direct comparative evidence to guide this decision is limited, and recent American Thoracic Society (ATS) and Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidance differ on when antibiotics can be safely withheld.

Objective

This project examines how the association between empiric antibiotics and mortality varies across clinically defined patient subgroups, and compares population treatment strategies: antibiotics for all patients, for none, the ATS guideline-based strategy, and individualized strategies that direct antibiotics toward the patients estimated to benefit.

Setting and methods

We studied emergency-department encounters with an initial diagnosis of pneumonia and positive chest imaging at 120 U.S. Veterans Affairs medical centers between 2022 and 2024 (130,265 encounters; 85.0% received antibiotics within the first 24 hours; 30-day mortality was 6.3%). Using a Bayesian Additive Regression Tree (BART) model with 89 patient factors, we estimated each patient's probability of 30-day and 90-day mortality with versus without empiric antibiotics in the first 24 hours.

To make the results clinically interpretable, we summarized these estimates within pre-specified decision trees built from six factors chosen for their clinical relevance to the antibiotic decision: pneumonia severity, severe hypoxemia or shock, hypoxemia or sepsis, lung disease, other comorbidities, and viral test result. Within each subgroup we report the average treatment effect (ATE) of antibiotics on mortality — the difference in the probability of death with versus without antibiotics — with a 95% credible interval. On this scale a negative ATE means antibiotics are estimated to reduce mortality.

Definitions

Pneumonia severity

Three levels, from vital signs, labs, and oxygen needs at the visit (SCAP criteria, ATS/IDSA 2007):

  • Severe — septic shock, severe hypoxemia, or at least three of: fast breathing, high BUN, low white blood cell count, low platelets, low temperature, or hypoxemia.
  • Moderate — not severe, but with sepsis, moderate hypoxemia, or a predicted mortality of 3% or higher.
  • Mild — neither severe nor moderate.

Hypoxemia

Three levels, from oxygen saturation, blood oxygen, and the oxygen support used:

  • Severe — on a non-rebreather mask, high-flow oxygen, CPAP/BiPAP, or a ventilator.
  • Moderate — a lower level of oxygen support, or blood oxygen (PaO2) below 60, or oxygen saturation (SpO2) below 90.
  • None — neither of the above.

Sepsis

Three levels, from vital signs and labs (the older Sepsis-1/2 criteria, not the newer Sepsis-3 definition; “sepsis” and “severe sepsis” are combined into one category here):

  • Septic shock — given a blood-pressure-supporting medication (vasopressor) within 24 hours.
  • Sepsis — not in shock, but with at least two signs of a systemic infection response (fever or low temperature, fast heart rate, fast breathing, or an abnormal white blood cell count) or any sign of organ stress (high lactate, high creatinine, low blood pressure, low platelets, or high bilirubin).
  • No sepsis — neither of the above.
